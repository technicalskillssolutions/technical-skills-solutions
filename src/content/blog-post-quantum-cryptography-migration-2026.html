Post-Quantum Cryptography Has Entered the Migration Phase

The immediate task is not to panic about quantum computers. It is to inventory cryptography, understand long-lived data, and prepare systems to replace vulnerable public-key algorithms safely.

Quantum computing is often discussed in headlines as a distant technology that might one day break today’s encryption. The practical security conversation has moved beyond that headline. On 27 October 2026, the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) finalized NISTIR 8547, a report describing its approach to transitioning from quantum-vulnerable cryptographic algorithms to post-quantum standards.

The report matters because cryptography is embedded in far more than a browser lock icon. It protects software updates, device identity, secure connections, digital signatures, certificates, backups and long-term confidential records. Replacing these systems takes coordination across vendors and infrastructure, often over several years.

What “post-quantum” actually means

Post-quantum cryptography (PQC) refers to cryptographic algorithms designed to resist attacks from both conventional computers and sufficiently capable future quantum computers. NIST standardized its first set of PQC algorithms in 2024. The best-known families include ML-KEM for establishing shared secrets, and ML-DSA and SLH-DSA for digital signatures.

These standards do not mean that a practical quantum computer can currently decrypt ordinary internet traffic. The concern is forward-looking: systems that must remain secure for many years may need to transition before an attacker has the capability to exploit older algorithms.

Why organizations are acting now

One risk is often called “harvest now, decrypt later.” An attacker may collect encrypted data today and keep it in the hope that a future capability can decrypt it. That makes the expected lifetime of information important. A document that remains sensitive for decades deserves a different migration priority from a short-lived public web session.

Another challenge is dependency discovery. A company may know that its public website uses TLS, but not know which internal appliances, old clients, embedded devices, certificate authorities, firmware-signing systems or third-party services rely on algorithms that need to change. NIST’s transition report is intended to help organizations plan these steps rather than treating PQC as a one-line software update.

What a sensible migration plan looks like

  • Create an inventory of cryptographic use: key exchange, signatures, certificates, VPNs, code signing, backups and embedded devices.
  • Identify data with a long confidentiality lifetime and prioritize the systems protecting it.
  • Ask software, cloud, hardware and identity vendors for their PQC support and migration road maps.
  • Design for cryptographic agility so algorithms and key sizes can be changed without rebuilding the entire product.
  • Test interoperability, performance, certificate sizes, hardware limits and recovery procedures before broad deployment.
  • Keep conventional security controls—patching, MFA, backups and least privilege—in place during the transition.

What individual users should do

Most individuals do not need to install a “quantum-proof” app or manually change encryption settings today. Keep operating systems, browsers, phones and routers updated; use supported services; and choose vendors that publish credible security road maps. Be skeptical of products that claim to make every file instantly quantum-proof without explaining the algorithms, interoperability and key-management model.

For developers, the main lesson is to avoid hard-coding cryptographic choices into a product in ways that make future change impossible. A migration plan is an engineering discipline, not a marketing badge.

The takeaway

NISTIR 8547 marks a shift from choosing new algorithms to coordinating a real-world transition. Quantum-resistant cryptography is not a reason for panic, but long-lived information and slow-moving infrastructure make early planning prudent. The organizations that begin discovery and vendor conversations now will have more options than those that wait for a deadline or a crisis.
