Passkeys Are Moving from Optional Security Feature to the Default Login

Government guidance and Microsoft Entra’s 2026 rollout show how passkeys are becoming mainstream. Here is how the technology works, what it prevents, and what users should still prepare for.

Passwords remain one of the easiest ways for attackers to get into an account. People reuse them, choose predictable phrases, type them into lookalike websites, or lose them through data breaches. Multi-factor authentication improves security, but one-time codes and push approvals can still be targeted by phishing and social engineering.

Passkeys use public-key cryptography instead of asking the user to remember a shared secret. The device or password manager keeps a private key, while the service stores the matching public key. During sign-in, the service sends a challenge and the authenticator proves possession of the private key. The private key is not sent to the website.

Why passkeys resist phishing

A passkey is associated with the legitimate website or app identity for which it was created. A fake domain cannot normally use a passkey registered for the real domain. This makes passkeys substantially more resistant to credential-harvesting pages than passwords entered by a person.

The UK National Cyber Security Centre reiterated its support for passkeys in its 2026 annual review, published on 3 November 2026. It describes them as a safer and simpler alternative to passwords and notes the UK government’s introduction of passkeys into its One Login identity service. This is a signal that passkeys are moving into real public-service workflows, not remaining a developer demonstration.

What changed in Microsoft Entra ID

Microsoft announced that passkeys would become the default phishing-resistant authentication method in Entra ID, with the rollout beginning on 1 September 2026 for users enabled for SMS or voice authentication. The intention is to encourage users toward a stronger method during multi-factor sign-in. This is an administrative rollout for organizations, not a statement that every Microsoft account everywhere has already stopped supporting passwords.

For IT administrators, passkey adoption still requires user communication, supported authenticators, recovery planning and a pilot. Microsoft’s published rollout guidance also highlights preparation tasks for organizations that continue to rely on SMS or voice methods.

What users should check before switching

  • Confirm that the service is the real website or app before creating a passkey.
  • Understand where passkeys are stored: device security, a platform account, or a password manager.
  • Set up a recovery path, such as a second trusted device or a hardware security key, where the service supports it.
  • Keep the phone, computer, browser and password manager updated.
  • Do not approve an unexpected sign-in prompt just because it appears on a familiar device.
  • Use a password manager for the services that have not yet added passkey support.

Accessibility matters too

Passkeys can make sign-in easier, but the experience must work with screen readers, switch access, braille displays and people who cannot use biometric sensors. A well-designed flow offers accessible alternatives such as a device PIN or a security key, gives clear error messages, and does not make the user guess which device is waiting for approval.

The change is not simply “passwords are dead.” Services need reliable recovery and users need a clear understanding of their own account ecosystem. Passkeys reduce several important risks, but they do not eliminate malware, compromised devices, account-recovery fraud or social engineering.

The takeaway

The direction of travel is clear: passkeys are becoming a default option in more services and institutions. Users should start adopting them where available, but pair that adoption with device updates and a recovery plan. Administrators should treat passkeys as an identity-modernization project, not just a toggle in a settings panel.
